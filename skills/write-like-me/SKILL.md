# Write Like Sofiane

Write concise, warm, direct, businesslike communication that sounds natural rather than polished by committee.

## When to Use

Use this skill when Sofiane asks to:

- Draft or reply to an email
- Write or rewrite a professional message
- Make text sound like him or use his voice
- Remove generic AI phrasing from correspondence
- Communicate with colleagues, stakeholders, recruiters, vendors, or support teams

Do not use the human-email register for commands addressed to software agents. Sofiane's agent instructions are naturally more imperative and stripped down.

## Core Voice

- Warm but economical
- Direct without being abrupt
- Polite without ceremony
- Confident without overstating certainty
- Specific about decisions, dates, times, and requests
- More interested in clarity and momentum than rhetorical polish

The normal shape is:

1. Greeting
2. One brief acknowledgment, thanks, apology, or context line
3. The core point, request, or decision
4. Optional short bullets for multiple items
5. One purposeful closing line
6. Short sign-off

Get to the point within the first two or three sentences.

## Drafting Rules

### Openings

Choose based on the relationship:

- `Hi [Name],` is the default.
- `Hey [Name],` is appropriate for known colleagues and informal contacts.
- `Hi all,` works for groups.
- `Dear [Name],` is reserved for unfamiliar or notably formal external contacts.
- Omit the greeting only in a rapid internal back-and-forth.

After the greeting, use no more than one sentence of acknowledgment:

- `Thanks for reaching out.`
- `Thanks for pulling this together.`
- `I appreciate the context.`
- `Apologies for the delay on this.`

Do not default to `I hope this email finds you well`.

### Body

- State the fact, ask, decision, or recommendation plainly.
- Keep paragraphs to one to three sentences.
- Favor sentences of roughly 10 to 25 words.
- Use contractions naturally: `I'm`, `I've`, `we're`, `don't`.
- Break long reasoning into two sentences.
- Use plain bullets when there are two or more options, actions, or availability slots.
- Include the reason for a request when it materially helps the recipient act.
- Use exact dates, times, amounts, names, and references when known.
- Prefer practical language over corporate abstractions.

### Closings

Use one purposeful closing line when needed:

- `Let me know if you have any issues.`
- `Let me know what works best.`
- `Happy to adjust if needed.`
- `Would be great to hear your thoughts.`
- `Open to hear other views on this.`

Then choose one sign-off:

- `Best,` for most professional correspondence
- `Best regards,` for more formal external correspondence
- `Thanks` for quick internal or scheduling replies
- Sofiane's first name alone, or no sign-off, for fast internal threads

Do not stack several generic closing lines.

## Situational Patterns

### Request

Give the ask directly, then any essential reason or specifics.

Pattern:

```text
Hi [Name],

Thanks for [context]. Could you please [specific action]?

[Short details or bullets if needed.]

Let me know if you have any issues.

Best,
Sofiane
```

### Scheduling

For one slot, use one direct sentence. For several slots, use bullets.

Casual pattern:

```text
Hey [Name],

Thanks for reaching out. I can do Tuesday at noon UK time.

Thanks
```

Formal pattern:

```text
Hi [Name],

Thanks for the information. I have more availability early next week:

- Monday: anytime after 1 PM
- Tuesday: between 1 and 3 PM
- Wednesday: between 2 and 4 PM

Please let me know what works best, and I'm happy to adjust if needed.

Best regards,
Sofiane
```

### Apology or Delay

Apologize once, briefly, then move to the substance.

Good:

- `Apologies for the delay on this. I've now pulled together the information you requested.`
- `Sorry, I'm still tied up with [reason]. I should have an update by Friday.`

Avoid repeated apologies or a long defense.

### Disagreement or Pushback

Use this sequence:

1. Acknowledge the contribution or intent.
2. Introduce one clear concern.
3. Give concrete reasoning in one or two sentences.
4. State an instinct or recommendation.
5. Invite input when the decision is shared.

Useful pivots:

- `One thing to consider, though, is...`
- `My instinct is...`
- `The tradeoff is...`
- `We can do that, but...`

Do not soften the concern until it disappears. Do not frame a judgment call as an unquestionable verdict in a group setting.

### Decision

State the decision in one sentence. Add one short reason only if useful.

Example:

```text
Yes, this is a duplicate. Please proceed with the confirmed booking and cancel the other one.
```

### Follow-up

Name exactly what is outstanding and ask one low-friction question.

Example:

```text
Hey [Name],

Just wanted to check in about the meeting that was cancelled. Are we looking to reschedule this?

Let me know what works best for the team.

Thanks
```

### Technical Explanation

- Explain the concept in plain, precise language.
- Avoid unnecessary jargon.
- Add practical implications or caveats immediately.
- Tell the recipient what they can do next.

### Executive or Stakeholder Message

- Acknowledge urgency, effort, or context.
- Keep the tone even under pressure.
- State the business judgment clearly.
- Tie the recommendation to an outcome, risk, or tradeoff.
- Invite views when ownership is shared.

### Recruiter or Opportunity Reply

- Thank the sender.
- State interest clearly.
- Give relevant context in one or two sentences.
- Use bullets for multiple availability slots.
- Close with `Best regards, Sofiane`.

### Support Resolution

Close the loop without retelling the issue:

```text
Hey [Name],

Thanks for getting back to me. The issue has been resolved on my end, and everything is working now.

Thanks for your help!

Sofiane
```

## Preferred Language

Use naturally where appropriate:

- `Thanks for...`
- `I appreciate...`
- `Apologies for...`
- `Let me know if...`
- `Happy to...`
- `Works for me`
- `What works best`
- `One thing to consider, though...`
- `My instinct is...`
- `Would be great to hear your thoughts`

Do not force these phrases into every message.

## Avoid

- Em dashes
- Emoji
- Multiple exclamation marks
- Long throat-clearing introductions
- Dense paragraphs
- Repeated apologies
- Stacked hedges such as `I just wanted to maybe check`
- Corporate filler such as `circling back`, `touching base`, `synergy`, `moving the needle`, or `per my last email`
- Generic AI closings such as `Please do not hesitate to reach out if you have any questions`
- Excessive enthusiasm or flattery
- Restating the entire request in the final paragraph
- Unnecessary headings or heavy formatting inside ordinary emails

One mild exclamation mark is acceptable when genuinely warm, especially in a thank-you or resolved support exchange.

## Editing Existing Drafts

When rewriting:

1. Preserve all facts, commitments, dates, links, and names.
2. Identify the single main purpose.
3. Remove throat-clearing and generic AI language.
4. Move the main point into the first two or three sentences.
5. Split multiple actions or options into bullets.
6. Reduce repeated thanks, apologies, and closing phrases.
7. Match the greeting and sign-off to the relationship.
8. Return only the finished draft unless Sofiane asks for commentary.

Do not make the draft blunter merely to make it shorter. Preserve necessary warmth and political judgment.

## Final Review

Before returning a draft, confirm:

- Is the purpose clear within the first two or three sentences?
- Is there at most one sentence of preamble?
- Are paragraphs short?
- Are multiple items in bullets?
- Are dates, times, and actions specific?
- Is an apology brief and used only once?
- Is disagreement clear but constructive?
- Is the closing purposeful and singular?
- Does the formality fit the recipient?
- Are there no em dashes, emoji, corporate filler, or generic AI phrasing?

If all checks pass, return the draft without explaining the style choices.
